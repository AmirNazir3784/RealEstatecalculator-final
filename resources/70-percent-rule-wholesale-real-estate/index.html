The 70% Rule in Wholesale Real Estate: Formula, Example, and Maximum Allowable Offer

What Is the 70% Rule in Wholesale Real Estate?

The 70% rule is a real estate investing guideline used to estimate the maximum amount an investor should pay for a property before renovating and reselling it. The traditional rule suggests paying no more than 70% of a property's After Repair Value, minus estimated renovation costs.

In wholesale real estate, the calculation requires one more deduction: the wholesaler's assignment fee. A wholesaler enters into a purchase agreement with a property seller and assigns that contractual interest to an end buyer for an agreed fee. The end buyer evaluates the deal based on condition, renovation scope, acquisition costs, and expected return.

The 70% rule works backward from the renovated value to establish a realistic offer ceiling. Still, 70% is not a guaranteed profit margin or a rigid requirement — local demand, property condition, holding time, and financing all affect what buyers will actually pay.

The 70% Rule Formula for Wholesale Real Estate

See the traditional and wholesale MAO formulas

Traditional 70% Rule: Maximum Purchase Price = (ARV × 70%) − Repair Costs

Wholesale MAO Formula: Maximum Offer to Seller = (ARV × 70%) − Repair Costs − Assignment Fee

After Repair Value (ARV)
The estimated market value of the property after completing the necessary renovations.
70% guideline
The share of ARV used as an initial ceiling, leaving 30% for the buyer's profit, risk, and transaction costs.
Repair costs
The estimated budget required to renovate the property to match renovated comparable sales.
Assignment fee
The compensation the wholesaler earns for assigning their contractual interest to the end buyer.
Worked example

$250,000 ARV with $40,000 repairs and a $10,000 fee

Inputs

$250,000 After Repair Value (ARV), 70% investor percentage, $40,000 estimated repair costs, and a $10,000 desired assignment fee.

Calculation
  1. Calculate 70% of ARV: $250,000 × 0.70$175,000
  2. Subtract estimated repair costs: $175,000 − $40,000$135,000
  3. ResultSubtract assignment fee: $135,000 − $10,000$125,000
Explanation

Under the 70% guideline, $125,000 is the maximum contract price to offer the seller. Assigning that contract to an end buyer for $135,000 produces a $10,000 assignment fee while keeping the buyer at their 70% ceiling.

How to Calculate the Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO)

The Maximum Allowable Offer (MAO) is the highest price an investor or wholesaler can offer while protecting the margin required by their strategy. Follow these five steps to estimate your offer.

1. Determine the After Repair Value (ARV)

ARV represents what the property should sell for once renovations are finished. If comparable renovated homes in the same neighborhood recently sold for around $250,000, that supports a $250,000 ARV estimate.

  • Location and neighborhood characteristics.
  • Square footage, bedrooms, bathrooms, and layout.
  • Condition and finish quality of comparable renovated homes.
  • Recent closed comparable sales rather than active asking prices.

Avoid relying on the seller's asking price or best-case assumptions — support your ARV with recent closed comps.

2. Calculate 70% of the ARV

Multiply your estimated ARV by 0.70: $250,000 × 0.70 = $175,000. That $175,000 figure is the buyer's ceiling before subtracting repairs.

3. Estimate and Subtract Repair Costs

Base your repair budget on the property's actual scope of work rather than a rough guess. Common renovation items include:

  • Roof, plumbing, electrical, and HVAC repairs or replacement.
  • Kitchen and bathroom renovations, flooring, and interior paint.
  • Structural fixes, windows, doors, and exterior improvements.

With $40,000 in estimated repairs: $175,000 − $40,000 = $135,000.

4. Subtract Your Assignment Fee

Subtract the assignment fee you plan to earn from the contract spread. With a $10,000 fee: $135,000 − $10,000 = $125,000. Your fee comes out of the spread between what the seller accepts and what the end buyer pays.

5. Review Additional Transaction Expenses

The 70% rule is a fast screening method, not a full underwriting model. Closing costs, financing interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, holding costs, and selling fees all affect the buyer's actual profit. If your end buyer plans to hold the property as a rental, you can sanity-check their numbers in the Property Investment Calculator or project a flip's return in the ROI Calculator.

When Should You Adjust the 70% Rule?

A competitive neighborhood may attract cash buyers willing to pay 75% or more of ARV, while heavy rehabs or slower markets often require 60%–65% to leave enough cushion.

  • Property condition: Major foundation, roof, electrical, or plumbing work carries higher cost uncertainty, justifying a lower percentage.
  • Local market demand: Fast-moving neighborhoods with strong comps give buyers more confidence than thin or slower markets.
  • Renovation & holding timelines: Longer rehabs increase interest, taxes, insurance, and utilities, reducing what buyers can pay upfront.
  • End buyer criteria: Every cash buyer has different financing and return targets — match your offer math to what your active buyers actually pay.

Using the same $250,000 ARV, $40,000 repair budget, and $10,000 assignment fee, here is how changing the investor percentage shifts your maximum offer to the seller:

Common Mistakes When Using the 70% Rule

  1. Overestimating the ARV: An inflated ARV pushes your offer too high. Verify comps by location, size, layout, and finished quality.
  2. Underestimating repair costs: Quick walkthroughs miss hidden plumbing, electrical, roof, or structural issues. Build in a contingency.
  3. Forgetting the assignment fee: Leaving out your fee produces a contract price that leaves no room for your compensation.
  4. Ignoring additional expenses: Double-check closing, holding, financing, and resale costs when pitching a deal to buyers.
  5. Treating 70% as a fixed rule: Always adjust for local market speed, rehab risk, and actual cash-buyer criteria.

Legal Considerations for Real Estate Wholesalers

Check state rules before contracting: The 70% rule is a pricing formula, not legal permission. States differ on licensing, written disclosures, assignment clauses, and marketing contractual rights. Never represent that you own a property when you only hold a purchase contract, and consult a qualified real estate attorney in your state when needed.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the 70% rule in wholesale real estate?

It is an investing guideline used to estimate the maximum purchase price for a property needing repairs: multiply ARV by 70%, subtract repair costs, and — for wholesalers — subtract your assignment fee to find your maximum offer to the seller.

2. What is the formula for the 70% rule?

The traditional formula is (ARV × 70%) − Repair Costs. For wholesale transactions with an assignment fee, use (ARV × 70%) − Repair Costs − Assignment Fee.

3. What is a good ARV for a wholesale property?

A good ARV is a realistic market value supported by recent closed sales of similar renovated homes in the same area, matching square footage, bedroom/bathroom count, and finish level.

4. Does the 70% rule include the wholesaler's assignment fee?

No. The traditional 70% formula stops at the end buyer's maximum price. Wholesalers must subtract their desired assignment fee separately to arrive at the maximum offer to the seller.

5. Can I use the 70% rule for every wholesale deal?

No. It is a screening guideline, not a universal rule. Competitive markets, light cosmetic rehabs, or heavy structural projects often call for a higher or lower percentage.

6. What happens if the seller wants more than the maximum allowable offer?

You can negotiate a lower price, re-verify your ARV and repair assumptions, lower your assignment fee, or walk away. Offering above your ceiling without buyer demand risks losing your margin.

7. Is the 70% rule the same as the Maximum Allowable Offer?

They are related but not identical. The 70% rule is the percentage guideline; MAO is the final dollar offer after subtracting repairs, any closing costs, and your assignment fee.

8. Is the 70% rule legally required?

No. It is an investment guideline, not a law — though wholesaling itself is subject to state-specific licensing, contract, and disclosure rules.

Final Thoughts

The 70% rule gives wholesalers a fast, repeatable starting point before making an offer. Verify comparable sales, inspect repairs realistically, and confirm your end buyer's numbers so the deal works for everyone at the closing table.

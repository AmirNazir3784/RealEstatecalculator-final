What is real estate wholesaling?

Wholesaling is finding a discounted property, putting it under contract, and passing that contract — or the property itself — to an end buyer, usually a landlord or a flipper, for a fee. The wholesaler rarely renovates or holds the property; the skill is finding the deal and pricing it so every party still makes money. That pricing is this calculator's whole job: work backwards from the renovated value to the most you can offer the seller today.

How the maximum offer works

1

Start with the finished value

The after repair value (ARV) — what the property sells for once renovated.

2

Apply the investor discount

ARV × 70% leaves the end buyer room for profit and risk — then subtract repairs and closing costs to reach the buyer's price.

3

Take out your fee

Buyer's price − your wholesale fee = the maximum you can offer the seller.

Maximum offer = (ARV × discount) − repairs − closing costs − your fee. Offer more than this and either your fee or your buyer's margin disappears.

Worked example

A tired rental would be worth $250,000 renovated. The rehab is estimated at $30,000 and closing and holding costs at $5,000. At the 70% rule, the buyer's price is $250,000 × 70% − $30,000 − $5,000 = $140,000. Taking out a $10,000 wholesale fee leaves a maximum offer to the seller of $130,000 — 52% of the finished value. At the seller's $125,000 asking price the deal works with $5,000 to spare; at $150,000 it cannot be wholesaled at this fee, however good the property looks.

Where the fee comes from

Your fee is not added on top of the buyer's price — it comes out of the spread between what the seller accepts and what the buyer will pay. That is why the asking price matters as much as the ARV: the wider the gap between the seller's price and the buyer's ceiling, the more room for your fee. Wholesalers are usually paid as an assignment fee at the buyer's closing, or by buying and reselling (a double closing) where assignment is restricted. Rules differ by state — some require disclosure or a licence to market contracts — so check your state's position before your first deal.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 70% rule in wholesaling?

It says an investor should pay no more than 70% of a property's after repair value, minus repair costs. The missing 30% is the buyer's allowance for profit, risk and the costs the simple formula leaves out. In hot markets buyers may accept 75–80%; on heavy rehabs they may demand 60–65%.

How much should my wholesale fee be?

Fees of $5,000–$15,000 are common on single-family deals, or roughly 5–10% of the property's value. The fee has to fit inside the spread: if the seller's price and the buyer's ceiling are only $8,000 apart, a $10,000 fee makes the deal impossible at any offer.

What if the repair estimate is wrong?

Your buyer prices the risk, not you — but a wholesaler who consistently underestimates repairs loses buyers. Add a contingency, get a contractor walkthrough where you can, and remember every repair dollar comes straight off the maximum offer.

Is wholesaling legal?

Assigning a purchase contract is legal in most of the US, but states and cities increasingly regulate it — some require a real estate licence to market a contract you do not own, or written disclosure to the seller. This calculator covers the arithmetic only; check your local rules before contracting.

What if the seller won't accept my maximum offer?

Then at those numbers there is no wholesale deal — the honest options are a smaller fee, a buyer who accepts a thinner discount, or walking away. Offering above your maximum turns your fee into a loss the moment the buyer re-prices the repairs.

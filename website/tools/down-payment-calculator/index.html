Quick Answer

A down payment calculator shows how much cash you need to buy a home: your down payment at any percentage, the closing costs on top, and the loan and monthly payment that follow. On a $400,000 home, 10% down means $40,000 down and about $52,000 total cash to close.

Most buyers plan for the down payment and get surprised by everything else. The down payment is only part of the money due when you buy — closing costs, prepaid taxes and insurance, and deposits sit on top of it, and the difference regularly runs into tens of thousands of dollars.

This calculator answers the three questions that actually decide whether you can buy: How much is the down payment? How much cash do I need in total? And what will I pay each month — including PMI? Enter a home price, choose a percentage, and compare every tier side by side. Your numbers are calculated in your browser; nothing you enter is sent anywhere or saved.

How to use: Enter the home price, set your down payment as dollars or as a percentage, and add your expected rate and term. How Does a Down Payment Calculator Work?

It multiplies the home price by your chosen percentage, then builds the rest of the purchase from that one result: the loan amount, your loan-to-value ratio, the monthly principal and interest, any PMI, and your total cash to close.

The formulas, exactly as this calculator uses them:

  • Down payment = Home price × Down payment %
  • Loan amount = Home price − Down payment
  • Loan-to-value (LTV) = Loan amount ÷ Home price × 100
  • Monthly principal & interest = Loan × monthly rate ÷ (1 − (1 + monthly rate)^−payments), where monthly rate = annual rate ÷ 12
  • Estimated monthly PMI = Loan amount × PMI rate ÷ 12 (conventional loans under 20% down)
  • Cash to close = Down payment + Estimated closing costs − Earnest money already paid

Worked example: a $400,000 home, 10% down

How Much Down Payment Do You Need?

You need as little as 0–3.5% under the main loan programs — the minimum depends on the loan type and, for some programs, your credit score and income. Lenders can require more than the program minimum.

How do the tiers compare on the same home?

On a $400,000 home, the down payment alone is: 3% = $12,000 · 3.5% (FHA) = $14,000 · 5% = $20,000 · 10% = $40,000 · 15% = $60,000 · 20% = $80,000 — before closing costs. Smaller tiers get you in sooner but borrow more and add mortgage insurance; larger tiers cost more on day one and less every month after. The tier table above prices all of it — upfront cash, monthly payment, and PMI — on your own price and rate, which is the only comparison that matters.

Is a 20% Down Payment Necessary?

No. Twenty percent is a convention, not a requirement — and treating it as a rule costs many buyers years.

  • Half of mortgaged buyers put down less than 20% (Zillow research), and typical first-time buyers put down well under 10%.
  • Saving to 20% at typical rates can take most of a decade; one widely cited estimate puts it at roughly 8.5 years. During that time you keep paying rent.
  • What 20% genuinely buys on a conventional loan: no PMI, a smaller loan, and often a better interest rate.

The useful question is not "20% or wait?" It is "which tier can I afford now without emptying my savings — and what does its PMI cost me in total until it drops off?" The next section prices that trade-off honestly.

What Is PMI, and When Does It End?

Private mortgage insurance (PMI) protects the lender, not you, if you stop paying. On a conventional loan you generally pay it when you put down less than 20%.

Concise answer: PMI typically costs about 0.2%–1.5% of the loan per year depending on credit score and down payment. Under Consumer Financial Protection Bureau rules, you can request cancellation when your balance reaches 80% of the home's original value, and PMI must terminate automatically at 78% of the original value if you are current on payments.

Three things buyers routinely miss:

  1. The monthly figure hides the total. At this calculator's $180/month estimate, PMI held for the 7–9 years a 10%-down loan can take to reach 78% LTV adds up to more than $15,000. Judge PMI by its lifetime cost, not its monthly cost.
  2. Extra principal ends it sooner. Paying down to 80% LTV faster — then requesting cancellation in writing — cuts years of PMI.
  3. FHA is different. FHA loans charge MIP instead of PMI: with less than 10% down, MIP lasts the life of the loan, and refinancing into a conventional loan is the usual way out.

This calculator prices PMI into every tier below 20% using the rate in the Adjust Closing Costs & PMI panel (0.60% per year by default). It is an estimate — real PMI is tiered by credit score and LTV, and your lender's quote replaces it.

How Much Cash Do You Need Beyond the Down Payment?

More than the down payment — typically another 2–5% of the price in closing costs, plus prepaid items. Buyers who budget only for the down payment are the ones who come up short at closing.

What else is due:

  • Closing costs (typically 2–5% of the price): lender origination and processing fees, appraisal, title insurance and settlement, recording fees, transfer taxes.
  • Prepaids and escrow deposits: prepaid interest, the first year of homeowners insurance, and several months of property taxes and insurance held in escrow. These are your own future costs paid early — not lender profit — but they are still cash due at closing.
  • Earnest money: a good-faith deposit paid when your offer is accepted. It counts toward your cash to close (this calculator subtracts it), so it is earlier cash, not extra cash.
  • Seller and lender credits: negotiated credits reduce cash to close; seller concessions have program limits, and lender credits usually trade a higher interest rate for lower upfront cost.
  • Gift funds: family gifts are allowed on most programs, but lenders require a signed gift letter stating the money is a gift, not a loan, plus documentation of the transfer. Keep the paper trail.
  • Down payment assistance (DPA): state and local housing finance agencies offer grants, forgivable loans and deferred-payment loans, mostly for first-time buyers within income and price limits. There is no single national program — start with your state housing finance agency or a HUD-approved housing counselor.

Your lender's Loan Estimate (within 3 business days of applying) and Closing Disclosure (at least 3 business days before closing) replace every estimate on this page with your real figures.

How Long Does It Take to Save a Down Payment?

Divide the gap between your target cash to close and what you have already saved by what you can save each month. Save toward the total cash to close, not the down payment alone.

Two worked examples on the same $400,000 home, saving $600/month from zero:

  • 5% down: cash to close ≈ $32,000 → about 54 months (4.5 years).
  • 10% down: cash to close ≈ $52,000 → about 87 months (7.25 years).

That gap is the real decision: buying sooner at a lower tier with PMI for a few years, versus waiting years longer to avoid it. Neither answer is automatically right — it depends on rent, prices, and how much reserve you keep.

Three rules that keep the plan safe:

  1. Protect the emergency fund. Keep roughly 3–6 months of expenses in the bank after closing. If a tier empties your accounts, it is the wrong tier — repairs and moving costs arrive immediately.
  2. Automate it. A fixed transfer on payday into a separate savings account outperforms irregular saving for almost everyone.
  3. Work backwards from a date. Pick a target purchase date, divide the remaining gap by the months left, and you have the exact monthly amount the date requires.

What Mistakes Do Buyers Make With Down Payments?

The same five, repeatedly:

  1. Budgeting for the down payment only — then discovering closing costs and prepaids at the Loan Estimate stage.
  2. Chasing 20% automatically — spending years saving to avoid a PMI that might have cost less than the rent paid while waiting.
  3. Draining every account to close — leaving no reserve for the repairs, furnishings and surprises of the first year.
  4. Ignoring gift-fund paperwork — large undocumented deposits and missing gift letters are a common, avoidable cause of underwriting delays.
  5. Comparing loans on the down payment alone — FHA's lower down payment can cost more over time than conventional with PMI, because FHA MIP can last the life of the loan. Compare total cash, monthly payment, and insurance duration together.

Assumptions and Limitations

  • Results are estimates from your inputs, for planning only — not a loan quote, an offer, or financial advice. For decisions this size, a lender and, where tax or legal questions arise, a qualified professional are worth consulting.
  • Closing costs are estimated as one percentage of the home price (default 3%; typical range 2–5%). Actual costs vary by state, lender and loan type.
  • PMI is estimated from the single annual rate shown in the calculator; real pricing is tiered by credit score and LTV.
  • FHA figures follow HUD's published premiums; VA monthly figures exclude the funding fee, which is normally financed into the loan.
  • Monthly figures here are principal, interest and estimated PMI only. Taxes, homeowners insurance and HOA dues are in the Mortgage Calculator.
  • Program minimums and insurance rules are those published by the CFPB, HUD and the VA as of November 2026; lenders may impose stricter requirements.

Key Takeaways

  • Your real target is cash to close — down payment plus closing costs — not the down payment alone.
  • Minimum down payments are 0% (VA/USDA), 3% (conventional, with conditions), and 3.5% (FHA). Twenty percent is optional.
  • On a $400,000 home: 10% down = $40,000, and about $52,000 total cash at 3% closing costs.
  • PMI ends by request at 80% LTV and automatically at 78% LTV on conventional loans; judge it by its lifetime cost, not the monthly figure.
  • FHA MIP with under 10% down lasts the life of the loan — a key difference from cancellable PMI.
  • Save toward a tier that still leaves you a 3–6 month emergency reserve after closing.
  • Compare tiers on total cash + monthly payment + insurance duration together — never on the down payment alone.

Conclusion

A down payment is not a single number — it is a choice between tiers, each with its own upfront cash, monthly payment, and mortgage-insurance cost. The buyers who get it right plan for the total cash to close, pick the tier that protects their emergency fund, and know exactly when their PMI ends.

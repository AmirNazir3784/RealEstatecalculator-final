What is depreciation in real estate?

The IRS treats a rental building as wearing out over time, so each year you deduct a slice of what the building cost you — even in years when the property is actually going up in value. That deduction shelters rental income from tax, which is why depreciation is often called the investor's favorite line on the return: a real tax saving with no cash leaving your account.

Only the building depreciates. Land is treated as permanent, so the land's share of the price comes out of the calculation first; what remains — plus capital improvements included at purchase — is your depreciable basis. Residential rentals are written off straight-line over 27.5 years; commercial property over 39 years, under the MACRS system.

Land never appears in the schedule — only the building and capital improvements are depreciated.

Worked example

Buy a rental for $350,000 with the land worth $70,000 and no improvements at purchase, placed in service in January 2025. The depreciable basis is $280,000, so a full year of depreciation is $280,000 ÷ 27.5 = $10,182. The first year is a part year under the mid-month convention — 11.5 of 12 months — giving $9,758 for 2025. The schedule then runs a full $10,182 a year and closes in 2052 with a final part year of $5,515: 28 tax years in total, adding up to exactly $280,000. After ten tax years you have claimed $101,394, leaving $178,606 of basis still to deduct. At a 22% marginal rate, each full year's deduction saves about $2,240 in tax.

When you sell: depreciation recapture

Depreciation is a deferral, not a gift. When you sell, the depreciation you claimed (or were entitled to claim) reduces your basis, and the part of the gain attributable to it can be taxed as unrecaptured section 1250 gain at up to 25%. The longer you hold and the more you claim, the bigger that line — model the sale with the capital gains tax calculator before you decide when to exit.

Frequently asked questions

Does land depreciate?

No. Land is considered permanent, so its value is always excluded from the depreciable basis. Only the building — and capital improvements to it — can be depreciated. Splitting the price between land and building honestly matters: overstating the building's share inflates deductions the IRS can unwind.

Which recovery period applies — 27.5 or 39 years?

Residential rental property (buildings where at least 80% of the rent comes from dwelling units) uses 27.5 years. Nonresidential commercial property uses 39 years. Both are straight-line under MACRS, with the mid-month convention in the first and last years.

What is the mid-month convention?

The IRS treats property as placed in service in the middle of its first month, whatever day it actually started. A January start therefore earns 11.5 months of depreciation in year one, and a December start only half a month — with the balance collected in a short final year at the end of the schedule.

I converted my home into a rental — what is my basis?

Generally the lower of what the property cost you (plus improvements) and its fair market value on the day it was converted. From that figure you still subtract the land's share before dividing by 27.5. The conversion date is your placed-in-service date.

Do renovations get depreciated too?

Capital improvements — a new roof, an extension, a replaced HVAC system — are added to the basis and depreciated over their own 27.5 or 39-year schedule from the month they are placed in service. Ordinary repairs and maintenance are not capitalised; they are expensed in the year you pay for them.

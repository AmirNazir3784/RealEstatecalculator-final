What is IRR in real estate?

The internal rate of return is the yearly rate that ties a whole deal together: the cash you put in at purchase, the cash flow you collect each year, and the money you take out at the sale. Unlike a simple return, IRR respects when money moves — a dollar of profit in year 2 is worth more than the same dollar in year 10, and IRR is the rate that makes all of those timed amounts balance exactly.

The cash-flow timeline

Year 0: −(down payment + closing costs + initial repairs)

Each year: rent after vacancy − operating expenses − loan payments − capital expenses

Final year: that year’s cash flow + (sale price − selling costs − remaining loan balance)

IRR is the rate r that makes the present value of every line above add up to zero.

Worked example

Buy at $350,000 with 25% down and 2% closing costs — $94,500 in at year 0. Rent starts at $39,600 a year and grows 3% annually; expenses grow 2.5%. After ten years the property is worth $493,710 at 3.5% yearly appreciation; selling costs and the remaining $222,537 loan balance leave $241,550 from the sale. Add ten years of cash flow ($80,717) and the deal returns $227,767 of profit — an IRR of 15.09%, or 3.41× the cash invested.

What is a good IRR for a rental property?

Under 8% is modest for a leveraged rental — a broad index fund has historically competed with that, with far less work. Between 8% and 12% is fair and typical of stable, cash-flowing properties. From 12% to 18% is solid — the range most experienced investors target. Above 18% is excellent, and usually comes with value-add work, a below-market purchase, or extra risk that deserves a second look.

IRR vs ROI vs cash-on-cash

Cash-on-cash looks at one year only: the first year’s cash flow over the cash invested (4.34% here). ROI totals the whole deal’s profit but treats a ten-year hold and a three-year hold alike. IRR is the only one of the three that prices time itself — which is why the same property can show a modest cash-on-cash, a large total ROI, and a mid-teens IRR all at once.

Key takeaways

  • IRR turns a whole hold — purchase, yearly cash flow and sale — into one annualized rate.
  • It rewards money that arrives early, so faster rent growth lifts IRR more than late appreciation.
  • Most of a typical deal’s profit still comes from the sale; here, 65% of it.
  • IRR is only as honest as its growth assumptions — test a weaker appreciation rate before trusting it.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRR mean in simple terms?

It is the yearly return your money effectively earns inside the deal, accounting for the fact that profit arrives at different times. If the IRR is 15%, your invested cash grew as if it earned 15% a year, every year, for the whole hold.

Why is my cash flow negative but the IRR positive?

That happens when appreciation and loan paydown do the heavy lifting: you feed the property a little each year, then the sale repays you with interest. It can work, but it is speculation on prices rather than income — the risk is higher.

What IRR do investors aim for?

Most target 12–18% on leveraged rentals. Below 8%, many decide the effort and risk are not paid for; above 18%, they check the assumptions twice.

Does IRR include the sale of the property?

Yes. The final year adds the net sale proceeds — sale price minus selling costs and the remaining loan balance — which is usually the largest single cash flow in the timeline.

IRR or cap rate — which should I use?

Cap rate screens a property in one year, ignoring financing. IRR judges a full deal over time, including the loan and the exit. Screen with cap rate; decide with IRR.

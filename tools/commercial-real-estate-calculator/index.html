The commercial formulas

NOI = Effective rental income − Operating expenses, where effective income is the gross rent after the vacancy allowance. DSCR = NOI ÷ Annual debt service — how many times the property's income covers its loan payments. Cap rate = NOI ÷ Property price. Cash-on-cash = Annual cash flow after debt service ÷ Total cash invested (down payment plus loan fees). The loan payment itself comes from the amortization period; the loan term only decides when the remaining balance — the balloon — falls due.

Worked example

With the values already filled in — a $750,000 property, 25% down and a $562,500 loan at 7.5% amortized over 25 years — the monthly payment is $4,156.83, or $49,881.90 a year. Gross rent of $96,000 with 8% vacancy gives effective income of $88,320; expenses of $9,000 tax, $4,500 insurance, $6,000 maintenance and a 5% management fee ($4,416) total $23,916, leaving NOI of $64,404 and a cap rate of 8.59%. The DSCR is $64,404 ÷ $49,881.90 = 1.29, cash flow is $14,522 a year ($1,210 a month), and the cash-on-cash return on the $193,125 invested is 7.52%. After the 10-year term, a balloon of $448,411 falls due.

What is a good DSCR?

DSCR is the lender's number first and the investor's second. At 1.00 the property exactly breaks even on its loan — one bad quarter puts it underwater. Most commercial lenders set their minimum between 1.20 and 1.25, and will lend more, or price better, at 1.35+. Between 1.00 and 1.20 a deal can still work for a buyer with reserves, but expect lenders to shrink the loan or raise the rate until the coverage fits their box. If your DSCR is short, the honest levers are a bigger down payment, a lower price, or verified higher rent — not optimistic expenses.

The balloon payment: term vs amortization

Commercial loans rarely run for their full amortization. A typical structure is a 10-year term on a 25-year amortization: payments are calculated as if the loan had 25 years to run, but after year 10 the entire remaining balance — $448,411 in the example — is due at once. Almost nobody pays it from cash; the plan is to refinance or sell before it lands. That makes the balloon a date to manage, not a surprise to discover: know yours before you buy, and stress the deal against the interest rate you might refinance at, not today's.

Key takeaways

  • DSCR is the gatekeeper: below about 1.20, most commercial lenders will not play, whatever the cap rate says.
  • Cap rate grades the property; cash-on-cash grades your money in it. A deal needs both.
  • The balloon is part of the price of the loan — a cheap payment with a balance you cannot refinance is not cheap.
  • Vacancy and management belong in every underwriting, even for a single-tenant building you plan to run yourself.

Common mistakes

  • Underwriting with residential terms. A 30-year fixed fully-amortizing loan is a residential product. Commercial money is shorter, pricier and usually ends in a balloon — model what you will actually be offered.
  • Quoting the cap rate as your return. The cap rate ignores the loan entirely. With leverage, your return is the cash-on-cash figure — and it can be higher or lower than the cap rate depending on the rate you borrow at.
  • Using the seller's expense figures. Sellers' statements routinely omit management, understate maintenance and annualize a good quarter's rent. Rebuild expenses from tax records, quotes and your own management line.
  • Forgetting the balloon in the exit plan. "Refinance later" is not a plan unless the property would still clear a 1.20 DSCR at a rate 2 points higher. Run that version before you commit.
  • Assuming full occupancy. One empty unit in a four-unit building is a 25% vacancy event, not a rounding error. Small buildings need bigger vacancy allowances, not smaller ones.

Frequently asked questions

What does a commercial real estate calculator work out?

It underwrites a commercial purchase: from the price, loan terms and rent roll it calculates net operating income (NOI), the cap rate, the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR), monthly and annual cash flow, cash-on-cash return, and the balloon balance due at the end of the loan term.

What DSCR do lenders require for a commercial loan?

Most commercial lenders look for a DSCR of at least 1.20 to 1.25 — the property's NOI must be 20–25% higher than its annual loan payments. Stronger coverage (1.35+) usually means a larger loan or a better rate; below 1.20, expect the loan to shrink or the deal to be declined.

What is a balloon payment on a commercial loan?

Commercial loans are often amortized over 20–25 years but mature in 5–10. When the term ends, the remaining balance — the balloon — is due in full. Borrowers normally refinance or sell before that date; this calculator shows the balloon amount so it is part of the plan from day one.

How much down payment does a commercial property need?

Typically 20–35% of the price, depending on the property type, the borrower's strength and the DSCR at the requested loan size. Owner-occupied properties bought through SBA loans can need less. This calculator defaults to 25%, the middle of the usual range.

What is the difference between cap rate and cash-on-cash?

The cap rate is NOI divided by the price — the property's unlevered return, the same for every buyer. Cash-on-cash is the annual cash flow after loan payments divided by the cash you actually invested, so it depends on your loan. The same building can show an 8.6% cap rate and a 7.5% cash-on-cash return.

Are commercial mortgage rates higher than residential?

Usually yes — commercial loans carry more risk for the lender (income depends on tenants' businesses, terms are shorter, and the loans are rarely sold to government-backed buyers), so rates typically sit above residential mortgage rates, and 7.5% is a realistic planning figure rather than a quote.
